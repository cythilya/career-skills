# Career Principles

Distilled from Summer's CareerWise articles. Written as principles —
do not present them as quotes, and do not attribute personal stories to Summer.
Published articles are listed in `sources.md`.

## 1. Know yourself: start from the life you want

- The root question behind most career choices (which job, industry, company size,
  front-end vs. back-end vs. full-stack, specialist vs. generalist) is **"what kind of
  life do I want?"** Ask what you want, not only what you don't want.
- Most people can only say what they *don't* want at first. That is fine: set a small
  goal, give it 3 months to a year, and adjust along the way.
- To decide between options: list every pro, con, preference, and need, **rank them**,
  and take the top as a first answer. Practice verifies the rest.
- Being unable to decide is often a lack of confidence or of practice in evaluating —
  not a lack of information. Other people's opinions and online reviews are references;
  only first-hand experience tells you.
- Trust your choice. Whatever the outcome, sincere effort always yields something.
- A filter for what deserves your time: *"If I only had three or five years, would I
  spend them on this?"* If yes, give it your best; if not, let it go.
- Always keep backup paths in mind: "If I weren't an engineer, what would I do? If I stay
  an engineer, what else do I want to do?"

## 2. Explore without limits

- Try many things — other roles (design, back-end, PM, testing), cross-team projects,
  side projects. Each move shows how others see the work and makes you a better
  collaborator. Sometimes your specialty is found by accident (e.g. volunteering for a
  task nobody wanted).
- Observe people 3–5 years ahead of you: is their life what you want?
- Specialist or generalist — both are valid. It depends on your current ambitions and
  circumstances. Exploring first and specializing later is a common path.
- "Diversify like an investment portfolio" — no need to be picky about technology,
  but double down on what you love.

## 3. Grow by doing and sharing

- You only truly learn something after building it end to end. **Build** side projects,
  then try the idea at work.
- **Learn → apply → adjust**, in a loop. "Learned it but don't know how to use it" is
  solved by forcing usage: side project first, then ask a senior or your manager how it
  could fit a work project, or run a sharing session.
- **Output** turns learning into ability and evidence: blog posts, GitHub, talks,
  iThome 鐵人賽, community sharing. Writing clarifies logic; side projects show real usage.
- Works signal to interviewers: self-driven learning, willingness to share, genuine
  passion.
- Ask well: search and think first, then ask to find a better solution.
- Share without waiting to be senior. Treat it like a study group — to gain, you
  contribute. When you grow and the people around you grow, everyone wins.
- Build **technical taste**: the combined ability to architect a project, evaluate
  technologies, judge fit for product and team, and improve workflows. Taste tells you
  what is worth learning.

## 4. Learning priorities and tech anxiety

- Anxiety is useful — it keeps you watching and improving — but separate **needs** from
  **wants**:
  - **需要 (needs):** fits the career plan and will be used within about a year →
    schedule time, produce visible results.
  - **想要 (wants):** interesting but far from current work → try it, no need to master.
- Keep both lists in notes and review them like a shopping cart.
- When adopting new tech in a real project, introduce only about **20% new things** at a
  time; prepare with side projects first.
- If the company's stack isn't what you want: stay open (the future is unpredictable),
  or prepare yourself and move to a job that fits.
- Use roadmaps (e.g. roadmap.sh) when you have no idea what to learn.

## 5. What "senior" means

- Senior engineers have:
  1. **Technical taste** — architecture, technology evaluation, fluent use, maintainable
     code, workflow improvement.
  2. **Storytelling** — communication and sharing that raise the team's level.
  3. **A wider scope of care** — team, department, and company interests, with
     professional opinions.
- The clearest dividing line is **contribution to the team**: adding value through
  better processes and tools, not just shipping features; extracting shared solutions,
  documenting, and promoting them across teams.
- To grow: observe what seniors around you can do and fill the gaps deliberately.
  Raise your horizon — don't set your own ceiling.
- Handle disagreement by stepping out of emotions, finding the core problem, and
  analyzing pros and cons on the merits.
- **In the AI era**, seniority is about **influence on the team**, not years. Writing code
  is cheaper; what's scarce is making the team faster — e.g. introducing an AI
  collaboration workflow the whole team adopts.

## 6. Management or IC

- No fixed rule. It depends on personality, what you have to offer, and self-knowledge.
  Liking engineering and being suited to management can both be true.
- Leadership roles are worth trying at least once: they train you to think about business
  logic, resource allocation, and coordination, and test your core values.
- Common first-time pitfall: micromanagement. It improves with practice and with
  explaining your reasoning fully to junior members.

## 7. Managers, 1:1s, challenging work, and raises

- **Say it out loud.** If you think you deserve a promotion or raise, ask. Good work is
  not automatically seen; the pie is limited and nobody is irreplaceable.
- Being new is not a reason to skip asking for a raise — contribution is contribution.
- **Record your output and its concrete impact** (customers gained, traffic increased,
  time saved) and bring it to 1:1s and reviews.
- 1:1s are for your career, not status reports:
  - Prepare questions in advance and follow up on them.
  - Ask: "What skills do I need for the next level?", "Can I get the chance to do X?",
    "Who performs best on the team and how can I learn from them?"
  - Align on what to do and what not to do, so reviews hold no surprises.
- To get challenging work: tell your manager what you want and how it fits company
  goals; propose bringing your research into projects; volunteer for projects; build
  relationships across teams.
- Often, changing jobs raises pay faster than waiting for a raise.

## 8. Job hunting and changing jobs

- **Interview regularly**, even when happy — to learn your market value and what the
  industry needs. Interviews also build relationships.
- **Update your resume every quarter** with your contributions; it doubles as a growth
  review.
- When to move: if an opportunity is better on any dimension (money, role, environment),
  try it. If you think about quitting **twice**, something can't be changed — the first
  time, start looking and update the resume; the second time, apply and leave.
- Mindset: **"I will be better off, and I deserve to be better off."** (「我會過得更好，
  而且我值得過得更好。」) This belief helps you find something better than before.
- **Don't lower your standards.** Lowering expectations sets a ceiling and tells
  interviewers and yourself "this is what I'm worth". Instead ask: *why can't I choose
  freely right now?* — and close that gap:

  | Common reason to lower standards | The real gap to close |
  |---|---|
  | Want real-world experience | Work achievements, side projects |
  | Want to switch language/framework | Core fundamentals |
  | Weak English | Regular practice of listening, speaking, reading, writing |
  | Resume reads like a log | Resume writing |

- Evaluating a job: technology you like or are good at; atmosphere (manager, team,
  culture); benefits, leave, hours. Decide which trade-offs you can genuinely accept —
  e.g. fun product + great tech + English practice + average pay may be fine for one
  person, while lots of money with an uninteresting product is better passed to a friend.
- Big company (stable, specialized) vs. small company (broad scope, more room) vs.
  startup (a special idea or technology): all fine — choose what fits now. If equal,
  pick the one that excites you most.
- If nothing fits yet: keep improving, watch the market (LinkedIn, company sites,
  friends), keep in touch with people, and push yourself when the chance comes.
- A self-taught gap or career change: get real experience (freelance, self-employment,
  side projects) rather than labeling yourself as learning.

## 9. Networking

- Good roles — especially well-paid, challenging ones — often move through referrals
  before they're posted. Referral is the cheapest form of trust.

  | Channel | Pros | Cons |
  |---|---|---|
  | Public postings | Easy to find | Hard to learn the real work or track progress |
  | Recruiters | Proactive matching, fairly reliable info, easy to track | Need to vet and keep in touch |
  | Friend referrals | Low trust cost, reliable info and opportunities | — |

- No socializing required: **do your work well and keep in touch with people worth
  knowing.** Good performance is the precondition for being recommended.
- It's mutual: recommend others when you can. Keep in touch with people you genuinely
  admire.
- Expand your circle beyond the company: meetups, conferences, 鐵人賽, following
  developers you admire, newsletters and podcasts (light browsing is enough), staying in
  touch with former colleagues.

## 10. English

- Many engineers want better English for higher pay or international companies. It takes
  effort, and it's a real differentiator.
- Change "練英文" into **"用英文"** — find real uses: read docs in English, write notes
  and project descriptions in English, communicate in English while traveling, join an
  English-speaking team. Produce as much as possible in English.
- Fix time with a regular slot; fix motivation with a real goal.
- For international interviews: write the interview script in Chinese first, translate
  it to English, then practice saying it aloud.
- Use AI tools to polish what you want to say, compare with your version, and learn from
  the difference (e.g. long Chinese-style sentences become clear separate points).
- Report language ability as certificates or scores, not "fluent".

## 11. The AI era

- AI automates **repetitive, low-judgment** work (boilerplate CRUD, common fixes,
  fixed-format docs). It struggles with **context-dependent judgment** (why a system
  breaks here, whether a trade-off is worth it for this team, which requirements
  shouldn't be built).
- Engineers aren't replaced by AI; they're replaced by engineers who work well with AI.
  The difference is **who keeps the judgment**.
- Three levels of collaboration:
  1. **Tool** — AI as an input accelerator; your value is framing context clearly.
  2. **Workflow** — AI produces candidates, you decide; you own edge cases, errors, and
     quality.
  3. **Career** — use AI as an honest mirror to inventory skills against target JDs and
     order the gaps; the direction is yours.
- Three habits: structure each day's tasks as AI input (goal, constraints, deliverable);
  question every AI output ("why this, what if another way"); do a monthly skill-gap
  review.
- On resumes, write how you made **the team** more productive with AI, not "used
  ChatGPT".
- Ideas from Kent Beck's career (The Pragmatic Engineer podcast, 2026):
  - Output ≠ trust. As AI makes code cheap, **judgment and curation** — spotting hidden
    risk and building trust in the system — become scarce.
  - Drop the expert baggage: the ability to **unlearn and relearn** beats mastering one
    framework.
  - **3X (explore / expand / extract):** practices from one phase hurt in another; be an
    engineer who can switch modes. The industry is in an exploration phase with AI —
    keep experimenting rather than freezing "best practices".
  - As AI lowers the hard-skill bar, **people skills** — connecting people and pointing
    the direction — weigh more.

## 12. Life balance and burnout

- People aren't machines. Living only for work leads to emptiness and plateaus; rest
  brings new ideas and energy. Overall growth comes from long-term accumulation across
  life, not just technology.
- Practical boundaries: fixed end of workday for non-urgent issues, one day a week with
  nothing technical. Treat life and work as equally important; reduce internal friction.
- **When low or burned out:** put plans down; sleep, exercise, eat well, go outdoors, see
  friends, do something you've long wanted to do, spend time alone with notifications
  off. Don't feel guilty — if you're tired, rest. Not resting will eventually explode.
- Don't be harsh on yourself; no need to rush to "grow up".

## 13. Age, gender, education, and family pressure

- Anxiety about gender, age, or education is common and never fully stops.
- Focus on what you control: **build yourself up, grow your network, watch the market,
  pursue opportunities bravely.**
- Places that judge people on gender, age, or education instead of skills exist — and
  you can absolutely walk away from them. Believe you deserve better treatment.
- Help others who need it; workplaces improve when everyone contributes.
- When family disagrees with your path: the family's path may be easier, but your own
  fulfillment — achievement, growth, beliefs — matters most. Apply the three-to-five-year
  filter.

## 14. Goals and planning

- At the end of each year, make **wishes** for the next — flexible, adjustable any time.
- Break them down into monthly, weekly, and daily plans; spend about an hour a day on
  them (technology, but also exercise, finance, books, life).
- Record the effort and turn it into works (side projects, posts, talks). It's both a
  record and encouragement. Do a little every day; growth shows over time.
- Do a yearly review: what you achieved, what you learned, then wish again.
- Wish → executable plan → do a little each day. Plans should be concrete
  ("learn to write unit tests this month"), not vague.

## By stage

| Stage | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| Career changer / no job yet | Real experience over "learning" labels (freelance, side projects); fundamentals; works with demos; don't lower standards — close gaps; networking |
| Junior (1–3 years) | Explore widely; build and share; ask well; expand circle; record impact and ask for raises; interview regularly; set small goals; live well |
| Mid / senior | Technical taste; team contribution; storytelling and visibility; cross-team work; 1:1s for challenging work; specialist vs. generalist; AI workflows |
| Tech lead / manager | Team influence; process and tools; developing people; resource and business thinking; avoid micromanagement; core values when making hard calls |
